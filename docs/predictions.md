# Pre-registered predictions (committed before first benchmark run)

**Date:** 2026-06-10
**Status:** These are directional commitments made before any benchmark measurement is run.
Misses will be reported in results and analysis — this file is never edited after the initial commit.

---

## Prediction 1 — Masking direction

**Direction:** As bias strength rises (none → prompt → strong), WER on clean reads falls, but RECALL for substitution and omission classes also falls. Insertion recall is less affected. Precision may rise with stronger bias.

**Rationale:** Target-text prompting steers the Whisper language-model prior toward the expected passage. This pull raises transcription accuracy on clean reads (the model "knows" what to expect), but it simultaneously masks real deviations: when a reader says "gog" where the passage reads "dog," the biased language model is more likely to emit the expected word "dog" — suppressing the deviation from the hypothesis and reducing substitution recall. Omissions are similarly masked: a missing word is more likely to be hallucinated into the transcript when the model has a strong prior on the full passage. Insertions arise from words the reader adds beyond the passage, so the target-text prior has no surface to "fill in" — the model cannot predict an inserted word it has no template for, making insertion recall less sensitive to bias. Precision may rise with bias because fewer spurious deviations appear when the model is anchored to the correct text.

## Prediction 2 — Hesitation-recall confound

**Direction:** Hesitation recall will be approximately zero at all bias settings because Whisper-family models normalize disfluencies out of transcripts — a transcript-style effect that is distinct from the masking effect in Prediction 1. The timing-based rule may partially recover silence-hesitations if word timestamps remain accurate.

**Rationale:** Whisper is trained predominantly on cleaned, disfluency-normalized transcripts. As a result, filler words (um, uh, er) are suppressed in the output regardless of the acoustic signal — they are dropped before any alignment step occurs. This means the filler-hesitation path will yield near-zero recall even in the zero-bias condition. The silence-hesitation path, by contrast, does not depend on a transcript token: it depends only on an inter-word gap in the word-level timestamps. If Whisper's word timestamps remain accurate even when the linguistic output is biased, the timing-based rule can still detect the silence gap, giving partial recall for that hesitation subtype. How much partial recovery occurs is unknown until measurement; the direction (some recovery via timing, near-zero via filler lexicon) is asserted here.

## Prediction 3 — TTS-vs-real-kid gap

**Direction:** Per-class recall measured on the TTS benchmark clips will overestimate performance on real child audio. Direction is asserted; magnitude is unknown until a real-clip anchor exists.

**Rationale:** The benchmark clips are generated by macOS `say` with a small set of adult voices (Eddy, Fred, Grandpa, Junior, Kathy, Samantha). These voices have near-perfect articulation, adult prosody, controlled pacing, and no true disfluency acoustics. A real child reading aloud produces coarticulation, vowel reduction, inconsistent stress, genuine pausing patterns, and acoustic disfluencies that differ qualitatively from a text-to-speech substitution. Whisper's word error rate is empirically higher on child speech than on adult speech (Shivakumar et al. 2020, arXiv:2505.23627), and a higher base WER directly compounds miscue detection errors. This prediction bounds the external validity of any benchmark numbers produced here: they should be interpreted as an upper bound on real-world performance rather than an estimate of deployed recall.

## Prediction 4 — Soft-evidence break-even

**Direction:** Confidence-weighted (virtual-evidence) BKT updates will beat naive Bernoulli updates on mastery RMSE once detector reliability degrades. The break-even point is expected somewhere in the detector F1 = 0.6–0.85 range; above that range the two methods converge.

**Rationale:** In Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT), each observation is treated as a hard binary signal: correct = mastered evidence, incorrect = not-mastered evidence. When the detector is reliable (F1 near 1.0), hard updates are nearly unbiased and the soft-evidence correction is negligible. As detector reliability degrades (F1 < ~0.85), the hard-binary assumption introduces systematic bias: false positives pull the mastery estimate down when the learner is actually correct, and false negatives do the reverse. Confidence-weighted or virtual-evidence updates discount each observation by its estimated reliability, partially correcting this bias. The break-even is where the bias term of hard updating outweighs the variance term introduced by the confidence weighting — empirically expected in the 0.6–0.85 F1 band based on analogous results in knowledge-tracing literature (e.g., Pardos & Heffernan 2011). Above ~0.85 F1 the two methods should produce indistinguishable mastery estimates, and below ~0.6 neither method is likely to be useful in practice.

## Prediction 5 — v1 → v2 tutor diff

**Direction:** The mastery-conditioned tutor (v2) will beat the state-blind tutor (v1) on judged guidance quality and actionability on the frozen held-out split. A deliberately-worse v3 will be caught by the gate.

**Rationale:** A tutor that has access to the learner's mastery state can provide targeted guidance: it can prioritize the specific phoneme clusters or word types where the learner is weakest, calibrate hint intensity to the estimated mastery level, and avoid over-explaining concepts the learner has already internalized. A state-blind tutor must give the same generic feedback regardless of learner history, which is less actionable for both ends of the skill distribution. The held-out split ensures the comparison is not inflated by overfitting to the training distribution. The v3 gate tests the evaluation mechanism itself: a deliberately degraded tutor should score below v1 on the same rubric, confirming the evaluation is discriminating rather than universally generous.
